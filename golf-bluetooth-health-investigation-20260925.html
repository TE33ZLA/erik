Can this Golf report its own health over factory Bluetooth?

An independent, evidence-led second review — 2014 VW Golf Mk7 (AU), Composition Media, MIB1 Standard, radio part 5G0 035 819 A.

Prepared 2026-09-25 · Second, independent investigator · Offline research + code/evidence review only. No live Bluetooth opened by this reviewer Shareable-clean: no device address or private identifiers Revision 2

  1. Demonstrated On this exact car, a Windows PC can pair and open the factory Bluetooth generic Serial Port (SPP, RFCOMM channel 5) with authentication and encryption — but across three owner-authorised captures the car sent zero application bytes, and no vehicle-health value has ever been retrieved.
  2. Demonstrated (research) The one publicly documented VW protocol for reading live vehicle data — EXLAP — runs on MIB2 HIGH over TCP/USB with per-user credentials. It is not a Bluetooth-SPP protocol and is not documented on MIB1 Composition Media, so it does not explain this endpoint.
  3. Hypothesised only Which application "owns" the generic SPP here is unknown. The discovery record is the standard, generic serial-port service with no identifying fields; VW's own documented profile list for this unit (HFP, MAP, PBAP, A2DP, AVRCP) does not include SPP at all; and "zero bytes" is equally consistent with a client-speaks-first app or an advertised-but-unused endpoint.
  4. iPhone reality A third-party iPhone app cannot open this Classic-Bluetooth SPP at all. iOS only exposes Classic-Bluetooth data links through Apple's MFi / External-Accessory program, so the Windows result does not transfer to iPhone.
  5. Working solution? No. There is currently no established, owner-authorised, factory-Bluetooth route to vehicle-health data on this exact unit. The missing piece is a primary source documenting a bound, documented read interface on this part — not another silent listen.

Bottom line up front: connectivity has been proven; a health-data channel has not. The evidence to date is best read as "we can knock on a generic serial door and it stays silent," which is the normal behaviour of an idle serial acceptor and tells us almost nothing on its own. The strongest honestly defensible position is that this endpoint is undocumented, so we must identify what it is before sending anything — and we do not yet have that evidence.

3 · What the live evidence actually shows — and what it does not

Three completed, owner-authorised, parked, 30-second captures (ignition/engine states: off, on-engine-off, engine-running) all produced the same result: connection established, authentication + encryption required, zero bytes received, no error. Raw SDP was identical across captures.

The central reinterpretation: "zero bytes" is close to uninformative

A Bluetooth Serial Port Profile endpoint is a raw, bidirectional pipe (RFCOMM emulating a serial cable). SPP itself carries no application framing and no "server announces itself" rule. In Bluetooth's own model the acceptor simply waits for the initiator. So a server that accepts the socket and then says nothing is the default, expected state of a serial acceptor whose application layer is client-speaks-first — or of an endpoint that is advertised by the Bluetooth stack but not wired to any data producer at all.

Therefore "30 seconds of silence" does not indicate a fault, does not indicate a health-data source, and does not identify a protocol. It rules out exactly one thing: a server-speaks-first banner protocol. (That single fact turns out to matter — see EXLAP below.)

What is genuinely established from the evidence: (a) the pairing, security and RFCOMM plumbing work; (b) the advertised services are stable; (c) no health field, fault code, or diagnostic response has ever been returned. Engine states are your reports, not decoded telemetry. None of this is a vehicle-health value.

The SDP record identifies no application

The Serial Port record you transcribed carries service class 0x1101, an RFCOMM channel, the Public Browse Group, and the name "Serial Port" — and no ServiceID, no provider, no description, no documentation URL. This is important: 0x1101 is literally the standard, generic Serial Port service UUID assigned by the Bluetooth SIG. Its presence is generic by definition; it names no owning application. ServiceRecordState 4 is SDP bookkeeping, not vehicle data. From the discovery record alone, the owning application is genuinely unidentifiable.

New in this revision: the SPP is the only advertised service with no documented job

VW's published profile list for Composition Media is HFP, MAP, PBAP, A2DP and AVRCP — SPP is not listed. Map that against what the car actually advertises: HFP (hands-free, ch. 1), A2DP sink, AVRCP, OBEX Object Push (ch. 3, receiving vCards), SyncML (ch. 7, sync) — each corresponds to a documented, user-facing function. (PBAP and MAP do not appear in the car's SDP because there the car is the client and the phone is the server; that absence is expected, not a discrepancy.) The generic Serial Port on ch. 5 is the only advertised service that maps to nothing VW documents.

Its record name, "Serial Port", is the Bluetooth-SIG default name a stack assigns when no application names the record. A bound application normally names its record — Apple's accessory link, for example, shows up as a named record ("Wireless iAP v2") and uses Apple-specific UUIDs, not generic 0x1101. This is moderate evidence, not proof, that nothing owner-facing is bound to this endpoint.

4 · EXLAP, checked carefully — and excluded for this endpoint

EXLAP (Electronic/Extended eXchange of Live Automotive data Protocol; also seen as "SAI-Server") is the real VW-group protocol for reading live vehicle data. I verified its shape against the best public documentation, the ijord/EXLAP-mib2-atlas guide and the edub0/EXLAP client. Four independent properties each rule it out as the owner of this Bluetooth SPP:

Property of documented EXLAPWhat our endpoint showsFit
Transport is TCP — "TCP connect 127.0.0.1:25010 … or the head unit's IP:25010 over its Wi-Fi/USB link." Also seen tunnelled over USB via an Android-Auto helper. Never documented over Bluetooth RFCOMM.Bluetooth Classic RFCOMM / SPP, channel 5.Wrong transport
Platform is MIB2 HIGH (and MIB2.5). The atlas title is literally "EXLAP on VW Group MIB2 HIGH." No source mentions MIB1 or Composition Media.MIB1 Standard, Composition Media.Wrong platform
Server speaks first: after transport setup the server sends <Status><Init/></Status>, then the client sends <Req><Protocol version="1" …/></Req>. This matches the "server-first Init" you noted from the 1.3b spec.Zero bytes from the server in 30 s, three times.Behaviour inconsistent
Credentials required: a per-user SHA-256 digest challenge/response (<Authenticate phase="challenge"/> → server nonce → client digest).Owner holds no such credentials; extracting them is out of scope by your rules.Out of scope even if present

The decisive line: documented EXLAP is server-first. A server-first endpoint would have sent an <Init> the instant the encrypted RFCOMM channel opened. Ours sent nothing, three times. So the silence is positively inconsistent with an EXLAP server on this channel — before we even reach the facts that EXLAP is TCP, MIB2-only, and credentialed. EXLAP is not the answer here.

Note the reconciliation you asked for: the atlas shows the client immediately following the server's <Init> with a <Protocol> request, which can read like "client-first." It is not — the ordering is server Init, then client Protocol. Either way, the server emits the first bytes, which we never saw.

6 · So who does own the generic SPP? Hypotheses, not a verdict

A generic SPP label settles nothing. Here is each hypothesis with the evidence for and against, kept open per your instruction:

HypothesisForAgainst / test needed
Phone / media / accessory link (e.g. an iAP/CarPlay-adjacent or media-control pipe) HFP is on ch.1, OBEX push ch.3, SyncML ch.7 — a media/accessory helper could plausibly use a spare SPP. Apple's iAP2-over-Bluetooth uses its own UUIDs (…deca-fade…cafe/caff), not generic 0x1101; and iAP2 exposes media/CarPlay handoff, not health. If it were an accessory link it would still be client-driven and media-scoped.
Synchronisation (contacts/settings) A dedicated SyncMLServer already exists on ch.7 (a known sync service). Sync already has its own endpoint; the generic SPP is unlikely to duplicate it, and SyncML is not health data anyway.
Service / debug / dealer port Embedded stacks sometimes expose a serial service/debug channel. VW factory diagnostics run through the OBD-II / gateway path (ODIS/VCDS over the diagnostic bus), not the head unit's Bluetooth. No public source documents a Bluetooth-SPP dealer/debug channel on this part. Probing it blindly is exactly what your rules exclude.
Vehicle telemetry (the hoped-for outcome) The endpoint exists and stays open. No primary source ties telemetry to a Bluetooth SPP on MIB1. The one documented VW telemetry read-out (EXLAP) is TCP/MIB2/credentialed and server-first — excluded above. Silence is consistent with "no producer bound."
Advertised-but-unbound stock SPP (the sober default) Perfectly explains: accepts a socket, requires the stack's standard security, then emits nothing regardless of engine state; identical SDP every time; generic 0x1101 with no ServiceID. Hard to disprove without observing a factory feature that actually drives the channel. This is the hypothesis the current evidence fits best.

Could the server legitimately be waiting for the client, a mode, or extra owner authorisation? (point 3)

Yes, in principle — a client-speaks-first application, a specific infotainment mode, or an ordinary owner authorisation step could all produce initial silence. But we cannot name the exact first message, mode, or permission, because no documentation ties a bound protocol to this endpoint on this part. Proposing bytes now would be blind command guessing, which your constraints (and good sense) exclude. The correct next move is to identify the owner, not to guess at it. A request being called "read-only" in EXLAP or any other protocol does not make it read-only, or even meaningful, on this unknown service.

7 · The data-scope ceiling (telemetry ≠ maintenance ≠ fault codes ≠ full diagnostics)

Even in the most optimistic world where some read interface existed on the head unit, it would be bounded by what the head unit itself knows. A Composition Media head unit sits on the infotainment/comfort side of the car's network. Stored powertrain fault codes (DTCs) and full diagnostics live on the powertrain bus behind the gateway and are read through the OBD-II path — which needs a plug-in adapter you have explicitly excluded. For reference, even the richest documented VW read-out (EXLAP on MIB2) exposes telemetry/status (speed, some dynamics, tyre, climate, nav) — not the engine ECU's stored DTCs.

Consequence: "engine fault codes / full diagnostics over factory Bluetooth" is not achievable on this architecture regardless of the SPP mystery. The realistic ceiling for any head-unit Bluetooth route is infotainment-visible telemetry/status — and even that is undocumented here. Anything not returned stays unknown — never "healthy" and never "zero."

8 · iPhone feasibility — assessed separately, and it is the hard wall

Windows RFCOMM success does not transfer to iPhone. The three iOS-relevant paths:

iPhone pathApplies here?Why
Bluetooth Classic SPP / RFCOMM (what Windows used) No — platform-blocked Apple does not expose generic Classic-Bluetooth SPP/RFCOMM to third-party apps at all. Per Apple's own QA1657, a third-party app is only notified of an accessory if it is MFi-compliant and communicates through the External Accessory framework. There is no public API to open a car's 0x1101 serial port from an app.
MFi / External Accessory (iAP2) No, for health iAP2 over Bluetooth is RFCOMM-based but requires the accessory to be MFi-certified and the app to declare a matching protocol string. VW uses iAP2 for media/CarPlay handoff, not for a health API, and no such health protocol string is published to third parties. An individual cannot obtain one.
BLE GATT (Core Bluetooth) Untested, but unlikely This is the only Bluetooth an ordinary iPhone app can freely use. But a 2014-era MIB1 Composition Media almost certainly exposes no BLE health service — the audited SDP found no ATT and no unclassified 128-bit UUID. An empty BLE scan cannot prove absence, yet there is no positive evidence for a BLE health GATT here, and no primary source suggesting one exists on this part.

iPhone conclusion: a native third-party iPhone app reading this endpoint is not feasible via Classic Bluetooth (iOS forbids it) and has no evidence for a BLE route. The iPhone question is not "port the Windows code" — it is "does an iOS-permitted channel (BLE GATT, or an MFi protocol VW would have to grant) even exist on this unit?", and today the answer is no evidence that it does.

10 · Strongest idea, weakest assumption, one next experiment

Strongest specific implementation idea

Stop treating the mystery SPP as a probable data source and instead identify it by observing the car's own intended behaviour — then, only if a bound protocol is revealed, look up that protocol's documented read request. Concretely: run a Bluetooth HCI/RFCOMM trace on the owner's own paired device (the existing Windows PC or the owner's phone — no added car hardware, no dongle) while methodically exercising every factory infotainment feature the manual lists (phone, message access, media, any "apps"/"data"/"online" item). Watch whether the head unit ever initiates or expects traffic on channel 5, and with what framing. This uses only installed hardware, sends no guessed vehicle commands, and either reveals the owner (then a documented request becomes possible) or shows the channel is never used by the car itself.

Cheaper first step (new): before any live session, open the two blocked sources listed at the end of §11 — the Bluetooth SIG qualified listing (which profiles Panasonic actually qualified for this unit) and the vagcode.info module entry for this exact part. If SPP is not among the qualified profiles, the "advertised-but-unbound" reading is close to settled without touching the car at all.

Weakest assumption

That the generic SPP is a bound application endpoint at all. The evidence fits "advertised-but-unbound stock SPP" at least as well as any active-service hypothesis. If that is true, no amount of client-side effort will ever produce data, because nothing on the car is listening or speaking there. The whole optimistic path rests on this one unproven assumption.

One next experiment (single, bounded, coordinated — not another silent listen)

Experiment: With the car parked and the owner present, and coordinated with the other agent so only one Bluetooth session is active, capture a full HCI/RFCOMM log on the owner's paired device across ~2–3 minutes while stepping through the documented infotainment features one by one. Do not send any application bytes to channel 5; only observe what the car does.

  • SUCCESS: a factory feature produces identifiable, framed traffic on channel 5 (or the head unit initiates on it). → We have the owning protocol; next step is to find its documented read request and prerequisites.
  • FAILURE: no factory feature ever touches channel 5 and the manual documents none. → The SPP is an advertised-but-unbound stock endpoint; there is no factory-Bluetooth health route on this unit. Stop pursuing this channel.
  • INCONCLUSIVE: a feature touches the channel but the app-layer bytes are opaque/encrypted. → We need that feature's client documentation before anything further; record exactly which feature and byte pattern for a targeted primary-source search.

If no route is established — the exact missing evidence

Rather than declaring undocumented access "impossible," the precise gaps are: (1) a primary source (VW/supplier documentation, an official client, or a reproducible trace on a MIB1 Composition Media unit) that binds a named, owner-authorised read protocol to this Bluetooth SPP; and (2) for iPhone, positive evidence of an iOS-permitted channel on this unit — a working BLE health GATT service, or a VW-granted MFi/External-Accessory protocol string. Absent (1), there is nothing legitimate to send; absent (2), an iPhone app cannot reach it even if (1) appears. Separately, the OEM online track (factory Wi-Fi + VW "We Connect"–style services) is a different investigation and, where offered, is manufacturer-account-based, not a local Bluetooth read.
